Materials checklist

Before developing, make sure you have everything you need:

Equipment

  • Developing tank (Patterson, Jobo or similar) + reel(s)
  • 35mm film cartridge opener: a dedicated opener, a bottle opener, or simply forcing it open
  • Accurate thermometer (±0.5 °C) — fever thermometers don’t work; they start at too high a temperature
  • Graduated measuring cylinder (500 ml or 1,000 ml)
  • Scissors
  • Funnel
  • Timer or phone
  • Opaque bottles for solutions

Chemistry

  • Developer (Rodinal, HC-110, ID-11, Caffenol…)
  • Stop bath (diluted acetic or citric acid) — we won’t be using this in the workshop
  • Fixer (Ilford Rapid Fixer, Kodak Fixer)
  • Wetting agent (Kodak Photo-Flo, Ilford Ilfotol)

Optional but useful

  • Changing bag: a light-proof bag for loading film onto the reel in daylight
  • Film clips for hanging
  • Latex gloves
  • Loupe for negatives

Where can I develop my black and white rolls?

Ideally you need a completely dark room. Completely means not even a trace of light — otherwise the film will be fogged. If you don’t have a suitable space at home (energy-saving bulbs emit residual light, doors let light in through gaps…), a changing bag is the solution: it lets you load the film onto the reel without any light reaching it. As a last resort, hiding under a thick duvet — preferably at night — will also work.

Loading the tank in darkness

Loading the film onto the reel is the only step that requires complete darkness. Practise first with an old, already-developed roll until you can do it entirely by touch.

  1. Start by washing and thoroughly drying your hands.
  2. Enter a completely dark space or use the changing bag.
  3. Open the cartridge with the opener (twist the metal bottom cap), with a bottle opener, or by inserting a finger into the film slot and pulling the cartridge apart. It’s not the most elegant method and care is needed not to hurt yourself, but it often works best.
  4. Pull out the film and trim the leader on the notched section, cutting it straight to make it easier to feed into the reel.
  5. Insert the film tip into the reel through the central slot.
  6. Use alternating rocking motions (back and forth) to advance the film, guiding it gently with your fingers.
  7. When all the film is loaded, cut at the point where it joins the cartridge to free it.
  8. Place the reel in the core, the assembly inside the tank, and close it tightly.
  9. You can now turn the light on.

Preparing the chemicals

Before handling any chemicals, it’s important to know they are toxic: not lethal, but they can cause skin and eye irritation and are harmful if swallowed. The first thing to learn is how to use them safely. Powder-free gloves, an apron to protect your clothes, and safety glasses to avoid splashes in your eyes are strongly recommended.

There are many types and brands of developers on the market. Each film brand tends to have its own developer that is supposedly best, but generic developers — Rodinal, HC-110, for example — work very well with most films. All these products come as liquid concentrates or as powder. The key thing they have in common is that they must be diluted in distilled water to work correctly.

If you want to use a stop bath, dissolve it in distilled water following the manufacturer’s instructions.

For the fixer, the standard dilution is 1+4, and it can be reused around four times — though mixing a fresh batch each time is always preferable. Always check the manufacturer’s instructions. Fixer is also available as a concentrate or in powder form.

Each developer indicates the permitted dilutions on the label.

Temperature: why it matters

Developer temperature is the single factor that most affects the result. Every degree of difference changes the contrast and effective sensitivity. The industry standard is 20 °C ± 0.5 °C.

Developing at different temperatures:

  • Warmer (e.g. 22 °C): the process is faster and more active. Reduce the time by 15–20% for every 2 °C above standard.
  • Cooler (e.g. 18 °C): the process is slower. Increase the time proportionally.

Pre-temper your solutions by standing the bottles in a water bath at the target temperature for 15 minutes before developing.

Agitation pattern

Agitation brings fresh developer to the film surface and removes reaction by-products from the emulsion.

Standard Ilford agitation:

  • First 60 s: continuous agitation (inverting the tank)
  • Every minute: 4 quick inversions (10 s)
  • Between agitations: tank rests upright

Stand development (for highly diluted developers):

  • 1 minute of continuous agitation at the start
  • Complete rest for 60–90 minutes (no agitation)
  • Result: extreme compensation, highlights preserved

The development process step by step

In complete darkness

  • Load the roll onto the reel.
  • Place the reel in the tank core, close the tank.

With the light on

  • Pre-soak the film with running water for 5 minutes.
  • Meanwhile, prepare the chemicals following the manufacturer’s instructions, always using distilled water.
  • Measure the temperature of the developer to determine the development time.
  • Empty the pre-soak water from the tank.
  • Development. Pour in the developer. Agitate continuously for the first minute; in subsequent minutes, agitate only for the first 10 seconds of each minute.
  • When the development time is up, pour out the developer. Rodinal, HC-110 and similar developers are single-use.
  • Stop bath. Rinse with running water 4 times to remove all traces of developer. This stops development gradually and gives a richer tonal range. If using a commercial stop bath, follow the instructions; diluted vinegar is not recommended.
  • Fixing. Add the fixer and agitate the same way as the developer: first minute continuous, then 10 seconds per minute for the remainder. Typical time: 5 minutes. Always check the manufacturer’s instructions.
  • Intermediate wash. Fill and vigorously agitate the tank with running water 4 times.
  • Hypo Clear bath (optional). Helps remove all fixer residues; thorough washing is sufficient without it.
  • Final wash. Leave the film in the tank for 12–15 minutes with running water. The tap doesn’t need to be fully open; the water just needs to be refreshed continuously.
  • Wetting agent. Add a few drops of wetting agent (usually one drop per litre). Leave for 2 minutes. Prevents drying marks.

Drying

Without touching the emulsion with your fingers, hang the film vertically in a dust-free space, using clips at both ends to keep it taut. Drying time depends on temperature and airflow.

Storage

Once dry, cut the film into strips of 6 frames (135 format) or 4 frames (120 format) and store in neutral polypropylene sleeves (not PVC). Keep the sleeves flat in binders or vertical filing systems.

Label each sleeve with: date, camera, lens, film stock, developer, and development time.
