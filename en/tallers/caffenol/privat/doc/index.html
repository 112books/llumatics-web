Base formula (for 500 ml)

IngredientQuantity
Instant coffee (not decaffeinated)14.5 g
Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃ — washing soda)20 g
Ascorbic acid (pure vitamin C powder)1.8 g
Distilled water to make up500 ml

This mixture has proven effective for developing standard black-and-white film in around 15 minutes, offering a good balance of density, detail and grain.

A brief history

Caffenol emerged as a homemade alternative developer based on everyday products — primarily coffee and vitamin C — combined with sodium carbonate. It became popular in the late twentieth century among photographers looking for economical and eco-friendly developing methods that could replace traditional chemicals.

Coffee contributes the reducing compounds needed to convert the exposed silver in the film into a visible image, while ascorbic acid boosts the reaction and sodium carbonate regulates the alkalinity, controlling the chemical activity.

This technique connects with the philosophy of experimentation and self-learning in photography, allowing exploration of different results depending on the choice of ingredients, times and temperatures.

Advantages of Caffenol

  • Economical: the ingredients are easy to find and inexpensive.
  • Eco-friendly: contains fewer toxic substances than commercial developers.
  • Versatile: allows adjustment of contrast, density and grain through small variations.
  • Experimental: ideal for learning photographic chemistry and taking control of the process.
  • Safe: using coffee and vitamin C means the chemical risk is much lower than with conventional developers.
  • Can also be used to develop photographic paper!

Disadvantages

  • Sensitive to temperature and ingredient quality, which can produce variations.
  • May produce more grain than commercial developers, especially at higher ISOs.
  • Less stable: must be prepared fresh and does not keep for long.
  • Longer development times compared to professional chemicals.
  • Not optimal for all film types; some require adjustments or test runs.

In our tests we have used the popular Fomapan in its ISO 100 and ISO 400 versions.

Development step by step

Prepare the workspace and materials

Work in complete darkness to load the film and place it on the spiral inside the tank. Have to hand: a precision scale, a lab thermometer (capable of reading 20 °C), a timer, film clips for hanging the negative, resistant containers and distilled water.

Solution A — Sodium carbonate

Place the 20 g of Na₂CO₃ in a container and add 250 ml of distilled water at room temperature. Stir until there are no lumps; a small hand blender works well to ensure complete dissolution.

Solution B — Coffee and ascorbic acid

In a separate container, dissolve the 14.5 g of instant coffee in 250 ml of distilled water. Add 1.8 g of ascorbic acid and leave to rest for 3–5 minutes until dissolved.

Solution A+B — Final mixture

Combine both solutions and top up with distilled water to 500 ml. Leave to rest for 10 minutes to stabilise the mixture before use.

Develop

  1. Load the film into the tank in complete darkness.
  2. Fill the tank with the Caffenol mixture.
  3. Agitate continuously for the first minute, then 10 seconds every minute until the end.
  4. Standard time: 15 minutes (adjustable according to the film’s ISO).

Quick rinses

Without removing the film, empty the Caffenol and perform 3–4 changes of water at room temperature, 1 minute each.

Fixing

Follow the instructions of the fixer you are using. Then wash with running water for around 5 minutes.

Final rinse

Do a last bath with distilled water and wetting agent / anti-static solution for 1 minute.

Drying

Hang the film with rubber-tipped clips in a clean, dust-free place. Once dry, it can be scanned or enlarged.

Adjusting contrast and density

To increase density and contrast:

  • Increase development time (15 → 18–20 min)
  • Raise the temperature slightly (20 °C → 22–24 °C)
  • Increase ascorbic acid (1.8 g → 2.2–2.5 g)
  • Add a little more coffee or carbonate (+10–15%)
  • More agitation intensifies shadows and contrast

To reduce contrast or soften density:

  • Decrease time (15 → 12 min)
  • Reduce ascorbic acid (1.8 g → 1.4–1.6 g)
  • Work at a lower temperature (20 °C → 18 °C)
  • Agitate less and, if desired, dilute the mixture slightly

Final result and scanning

Once the negative has been developed with Caffenol, we can make a print on paper using the enlarger just as we would with any black-and-white negative.

Or, if we want to keep experimenting, we can explore the possibilities of scanning.

Scanning: black and white vs. colour

To continue experimenting you will need a scanner with a negative module; a standard flatbed scanner will not work. There are many options on the market at a wide range of quality levels.

All of them let you choose the negative type: positive (slide) or negative, and black-and-white or colour. For a standard black-and-white image, select “Black and white”. But we suggest you try the “Colour” option: the results are spectacular.

Using the colour option, and depending on the white balance or manual exposure adjustment — varying by brand and model — you can obtain incredible reddish, orange and brown tones.

Why do these warm tones appear?

There are several theories, but the most plausible is that the Caffenol process leaves tiny traces of organic matter (coffee tannins and ascorbic acid) in the film emulsion. When scanning, these compounds absorb some of the blue and green light from the scanner and reflect more of the red range, generating that characteristic warm tone.

Whatever the cause, it is an effect that is hard to control and depends heavily on the scanner settings: it can be subtle or utterly brazen. Either way, the results never leave you indifferent.
