Prussian blue is one of the oldest pigments in photography and also the cheapest to make: two iron salts, water and light. In 1842, [John Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Herschel) mixed ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide and found that where ultraviolet light fell the paper turned blue, while the rest washed away in water. No silver, no darkroom and no developer: an exposure and a tray. Herschel wanted a way to copy notes and drawings, and for decades the process was used for the engineering plans that are still called *blueprints*.

The following year, the English botanist [Anna Atkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Atkins) used the same blue for something else: cataloguing the algae of the British coast. Between 1843 and 1853 she published *Photographs of British Algae: Cyanotype Impressions* in parts, with hundreds of plates made by laying each plant directly on the sensitised paper. It is what we now call a photogram, and that work is considered the first book illustrated with photographic images in history. The plates were a deep blue, and the plants appear with a precision no drawing could have matched: it is the paper that has been drawn by light.

On **Friday 11 December**, from 6 to 9 pm, we spend the afternoon on this technique at Nau Bostik. No previous experience or camera needed: cyanotype is photography without a camera, and we use it to print Christmas postcards, one at a time.

## How the afternoon will go

1. **Introduction.** We start by looking at prints made with this technique and placing the two people who made it possible: Herschel and Atkins. A few minutes, to know what we have in our hands.
2. **The formula.** We weigh and mix the two solutions —green ferric ammonium citrate and potassium ferricyanide, in equal parts— in dim light. It is the same mixture as more than a hundred and eighty years ago.
3. **Sensitising the card.** With a brush we spread a thin coat on watercolour card; the paper turns yellow-green. It has to dry in the dark.
4. **The compositions.** While they dry, we build the photograms: leaves, lace, paper cut-outs, acetate negatives. This is the part where each person decides which postcard they want.
5. **The exposure.** In the studio, with a UV lamp, or outside if the December sun gives enough —and it takes more minutes than it seems.
6. **The development.** Water and that's it. Five minutes of rinsing and the Prussian blue appears; the colour deepens further as it dries.

## What you take home

Everyone leaves with their own set of postcards and with the recipe written down. Cyanotype needs no darkroom: two salts, paper, light and a tray of water. With what you learn in three hours you can make as many as you like at home, and give them all away.

If you want to go further —negatives, large formats and toning that turns the blue into sepia or olive green— the [cyanotype workshop](/en/tallers/cianotipia/) is the next step. And if you want to start with a shorter taste of it, there is [photograms with cyanotype](/en/tallers/fotogrames-cianotipia/).
