## Wineol, base formula (for 500 ml)

| Ingredient | Quantity |
|---|---|
| Red wine (doesn't need to be expensive, but must have tannins. Avoid mass-produced wines with heavy chemical treatment) | 500 ml |
| Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃ — washing soda) | 54 g |
| Ascorbic acid (pure vitamin C powder) | 16 g |
| Common salt | 10 g |

<br>

Red wine completely replaces water: it is both the solvent and the main developing agent. The standard development time is **20 minutes at 22 °C** for ISO 400 film.

## A brief history

Wineol is the oenological variation of the homemade developer. Where Caffenol uses coffee — caffeic acid — as a source of reducing compounds, Wineol draws on the **tannins in red wine** — polyphenolic compounds found in grape skins and oak barrels — to reduce the silver halides in the emulsion to metallic silver.

Like Caffenol, it emerged from the community of experimental photographers seeking homemade, eco-friendly developers with unexpected results. Wine delivers a different tonal palette and very pronounced warm tones, especially when scanned in colour.

Common salt acts as a modifier: it helps control fog and slightly stabilises the developer's activity.

## Advantages of Wineol

- **Economical**: a cheap red wine is the main ingredient; the rest are pharmacy or hardware store products.
- **Eco-friendly**: low environmental impact, natural ingredients.
- **Unique tones**: wine delivers a colouration and nuances impossible to achieve with any conventional developer.
- **Experimental**: every wine is different; results change with grape variety, tannin level and alcohol content.
- **Can also be used to develop photographic paper!**

## Disadvantages

- Very sensitive to wine quality: a wine without tannins will give poor or no results.
- Less predictable than Caffenol: variations between wines make reproducibility difficult.
- Long development times (20 min) and slightly higher temperature than standard (22 °C).
- The negative may show organic residue if rinsing is insufficient.
- Not recommended for low-ISO films under high tonal-quality demands.

## Development step by step

### Prepare the workspace and materials

Work in **complete darkness** to load the film and place it on the spiral inside the tank. Have to hand: a precision scale, a lab thermometer, a timer, film clips for hanging the negative, resistant containers.

> The wine must be at **22 °C** before mixing. Check the temperature with the thermometer.

### Preparing the Wineol

Pour the 500 ml of red wine into a container. Dissolve the sodium carbonate and vitamin C in the wine, stirring well until there are no lumps. Add the salt and stir again. No resting needed: Wineol can be used immediately.

### Develop

1. Load the film into the tank in complete darkness.
2. Fill the tank with the Wineol at 22 °C.
3. Agitate **continuously for the first minute**.
4. Give the tank **4 firm taps** to dislodge any air bubbles.
5. From the second minute onwards: agitate for the **first 10 seconds of each minute**, rest for the remainder.
6. Standard time: **20 minutes** for ISO 400.

### Rinse

Without removing the film, empty the Wineol and perform **3 changes of tap water** over **2 minutes** in total to remove the chemicals.

### Fixing

Fix with a conventional black-and-white fixer for **5 minutes**:
- Agitate **continuously for the first minute**
- From the second minute onwards: agitate for the **first 10 seconds of each minute**

### Second rinse

Perform **3 changes of tap water** over **2 minutes** to remove the fixer.

### Final wash

Wash with **running water for 12 minutes**. This longer wash is important to completely remove organic wine residues from the emulsion.

## Notes on the wine

Not all wine works equally well. For best results:

- **Use a tannic red wine**: grenache, tempranillo, monastrell, merlot. Avoid light or rosé wines.
- **It doesn't need to be expensive**: a low-range wine with body and deep colour is ideal.
- **Avoid wines with excessive sulphites**: these can interfere with the chemical reaction.
- **Temperature is key**: at 22 °C the process is stable; below that it can slow down significantly.

## Final result and scanning

A negative developed with Wineol looks different from conventional developments: it may show a slight violet or brownish tint visible when held up to light. This is normal and does not affect image quality.

### Colour scanning

As with Caffenol, scanning in **colour** mode reveals surprising warm and reddish tones. With Wineol, the colouration tends to be even more intense, with violet and purple hues characteristic of wine pigments.

Experiment with white balance and scanner exposure to obtain radically different results from the same negative.
